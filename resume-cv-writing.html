A PRACTICAL GUIDE

Choose a format that fits the purpose

“Resume” and “CV” do not mean exactly the same thing in every country, profession, or academic setting. A hiring organization’s instructions and the conventions of the target market matter more than a universal template. If an employer specifies a format or length, follow that instruction unless you have a good reason to clarify it.

Choose the simplest format that makes the relevant evidence easy to find. The document can be adapted for a particular country, industry, or application, but legal and immigration advice is outside this service.

What each section needs to do

Sections should answer the reader’s questions rather than fill space. Keep a section when it helps establish fit, scope, capability, or context; omit or shorten it when it does not support the application.

  • Contact information: Include a professional name and a reliable contact route. Add location or work eligibility only when useful and accurate; avoid unnecessary personal details.
  • Headline or summary: State your professional focus and relevant strengths in plain language. It is an orientation, not a collection of unsubstantiated adjectives.
  • Employment history: Identify organizations, roles, and dates consistently. Explain scope and progression where they help the reader understand your contribution.
  • Achievements: Show what changed, improved, or was delivered through your work, with evidence that can be substantiated. Do not turn every responsibility into an invented result.
  • Skills and technical competencies: Prioritize capabilities relevant to the target and supported by actual use. Grouping can improve scanning, but a long keyword inventory weakens meaning.
  • Education and credentials: Include relevant qualifications and their correct status. Distinguish completed credentials from courses in progress.
  • Certifications and professional development: Include current, relevant credentials and the issuing body where appropriate; do not imply a course is a license.
  • Projects: Describe selected work when it demonstrates capability not already clear from employment history. Clarify your role and team context.
  • Publications and research: Use a selected list or discipline-appropriate bibliography. Follow applicable citation conventions and include only accurate publication details.
  • Awards and recognition: Give enough context to explain what was recognized and by whom. Omit items that are not relevant or cannot be verified.
  • Leadership, volunteering, and community work: Include contributions that show relevant responsibility, service, or expertise, especially when they add context beyond paid roles.
  • Languages and additional sections: State proficiency honestly and use a consistent scale if one is appropriate. Other sections—such as patents, presentations, board service, or interests—belong only when they help this reader.

Early-career applicants may give more space to education, projects, internships, and transferable experience. Experienced professionals usually benefit from prioritizing recent, relevant work rather than listing every task from every role. Academic CVs may require a much fuller record.

Turn responsibilities into evidence, not claims

A responsibility tells the reader what belonged to a role. An achievement explains a contribution, the conditions in which it happened, and evidence of what followed. The second is more useful only when the details are true and attributable to you.

REFINING A RESUME STATEMENT

Duty-only: “Responsible for onboarding new team members.”

Evidence-led structure: “Contributed to improving [process] by [specific action], supporting [verifiable change].”

Before using it: Replace each bracketed phrase with accurate details you can support, clarify your contribution, and avoid implying a measured result that was not recorded.

When reliable figures exist, identify their source and your contribution. If you do not have a defensible number, describe the observable change, decision, deliverable, risk addressed, or stakeholder need instead. “Improved performance by 40%” is not stronger than a careful qualitative account if the percentage cannot be supported.

A useful working note for each example is: context (what needed attention), your action (what you did), scope (your authority or contribution), and evidence (what can be confirmed). Use only the parts that help a reader understand the work.

A practical role-to-evidence review

Tailoring begins with understanding the role, not copying its wording. Separate essential requirements from preferred ones, then match each requirement to credible evidence. A gap should be recognized rather than concealed.

  1. Read the full description: Note responsibilities, required qualifications, level of decision-making, relevant tools, and the problems the role is expected to address.
  2. Group the requirements: Separate essential criteria from preferred skills and contextual details. Do not assume a keyword is a qualification.
  3. Map evidence: For each important requirement, identify a role, project, result, credential, or example that supports it. Mark evidence as direct, transferable, or currently missing.
  4. Prioritize: Give the most relevant verified evidence prominence in the summary and recent experience. Remove detail that distracts from the target.
  5. Check genuine gaps: If a mandatory license, qualification, or experience is absent, do not suggest otherwise. Decide whether to apply, explain a transferable equivalent where permitted, or seek a different target.
  6. Review the final version: Compare it with both your source records and the role requirements. The document must remain accurate and readable as well as relevant.

What to know about ATS and AI-assisted review

Applicant-tracking systems are not one universal test. Employers use different products and configurations for storing applications, parsing documents, supporting recruiter workflows, searching records, filtering information, or coordinating hiring. Some systems offer ranking, matching, or AI-assisted functions; others rely on different workflows. Human review and employer selection criteria also vary.

Use terminology that accurately describes your skills and work, especially when it is the same language used in the relevant profession. Do not stuff keywords, hide text, paste a job description into the document, or claim tools and credentials you do not have. These practices make the document less trustworthy and may be misleading.

Use clear section labels, a readable text hierarchy, consistent dates, and a file format accepted by the employer. Tables, columns, graphics, headers, footers, and PDFs are not automatically rejected by every system, but complex layouts can create extraction or reading-order problems in some workflows. When an application gives a format instruction, follow it. If you are unsure, a simple text-first version can be a practical alternative—not a guarantee of successful parsing.

No writer can promise a particular system will parse a document perfectly, rank it highly, or lead to an interview. A résumé supports a hiring process; it does not control the process.

Adapt the document to your situation

  • Early-career applicants: Use relevant study, projects, internships, volunteering, and transferable work. Be clear about the level of responsibility and do not present coursework as employment.
  • Career changers: Connect transferable experience to the target, but do not imply that adjacent experience equals a missing required qualification.
  • Employment gaps or return to work: Keep dates honest. Add concise context only if it helps the reader; a private explanation is not always necessary on the document.
  • International applications: Check the employer’s conventions for document terminology, length, personal details, and credentials. Requirements vary; this service does not determine immigration or work authorization.
  • Technical and specialist professionals: Prioritize relevant systems, methods, certifications, and work samples, while protecting confidential or proprietary information.
  • Managers and executives: Clarify decision scope, organizational context, leadership contribution, and substantiated outcomes. Avoid vague claims that cannot be connected to specific work.
  • Academic and research careers: Follow field-specific expectations for publications, teaching, grants, methods, and service; distinguish accepted, published, and in-progress work.
  • Extensive experience: Use selective detail and a relevant career narrative. Older roles can be summarized where appropriate, without misrepresenting dates or continuity.

A final quality check before sharing

  • Does the opening make your intended professional direction understandable?
  • Are role titles, organizations, dates, qualifications, and claims factually consistent?
  • Can a reader distinguish your contribution from a team’s work?
  • Does each prominent achievement have evidence, context, and appropriate attribution?
  • Are the most relevant requirements supported by visible examples?
  • Are section labels, chronology, punctuation, spacing, and date formats consistent?
  • Is the type readable on screen and on a printed page, with a clear heading hierarchy?
  • Does the saved file match the application instructions and preserve selectable text where needed?
  • Have you checked links, contact details, spelling, and the final exported file?
  • Have confidential, personal, or restricted details been removed or approved for sharing?

This checklist is a practical editing aid, not an ATS test or prediction of hiring success.
