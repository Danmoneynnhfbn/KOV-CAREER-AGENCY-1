# Global Jobs, Industries, and Recruiter Evaluation: Research Brief

**Prepared:** 22 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Research foundation for KOV Career Agency's role and industry directories and candidate-facing career guidance.  
**Scope note:** No single, definitive inventory contains every job title used worldwide. This brief explains how to build a defensible, internationally useful taxonomy and how recruiting evaluation commonly varies by work, employer, industry, seniority, and location. It is a research synthesis, not a promise about every recruiter or a legal opinion.

## Executive summary

“Job title,” “occupation,” and “industry” describe different things:

- A **job** is work performed by a person for an employer or in self-employment.
- An **occupation** groups jobs whose main tasks are similar.
- A **job title** is the label a particular employer or market uses; it can be local, branded, inflated, abbreviated, or unlike the standardized occupation name.
- An **industry** classifies the economic activity of the employer or establishment, not the worker's occupation.

As a result, a data analyst in a hospital, a bank, a government department, and a software company can share an occupation family while facing different domain expectations. “Director” may mean a senior executive in one market and a mid-level role in another. Titles alone are weak evidence of level or transferable fit.

Use **ILO ISCO-08** as the international occupation backbone; map local titles and aliases into it where feasible. Retain the site's existing **U.S. SOC 2018** detail for its 23 occupation major groups and use **O\*NET** for U.S.-specific tasks, skills, and work context. For industries, use **UN ISIC** for international comparability and preserve the site's **2022 NAICS** hierarchy as a U.S./North American view. Never label the U.S. catalogs “all jobs in the world.”

Recruiters typically work from a vacancy's requirements and evidence, not from an abstract ranking of professions. The strongest candidate positioning makes the target function, scope, domain, outcomes, tools, credentials, and location clear. What counts as credible evidence differs: a licensed qualification for regulated practice, a portfolio or work sample for output-based work, quantified outcomes for commercial/operational roles, and people, budget, risk, and decision scope for leadership.

## 1. A practical model of work

Represent a target opportunity as a set of separate dimensions:

| Dimension | What it describes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Occupation / function | Main kind of work and task family | Software development |
| Job title | Employer's label for the vacancy | Senior Platform Engineer |
| Level / scope | Autonomy, complexity, decision rights, and impact | Individual contributor, senior |
| Industry / domain | Employer's activity and operating context | Financial services |
| Specialty | Narrow subject or product area | Cloud infrastructure |
| Skills and tools | Demonstrable capabilities needed | Kubernetes, incident response |
| Credentials | Required or preferred qualification, license, clearance | State nursing license |
| Work arrangement | Employment form and working conditions | Permanent, shift-based, hybrid |
| Geography | Labor market and legal jurisdiction | Riyadh, Saudi Arabia |

These fields should not be collapsed into one taxonomy. Taxonomies support search, comparison, workforce statistics, and occupational exploration; the hiring organization still defines the actual job. A title crosswalk is a search aid, not proof that two jobs are equivalent.

### Why “all titles” cannot be a fixed list

Titles change by language, country, employer size, regulation, sector, and time. Employers create titles for internal grades, products, or branding. The same title can describe different work; similar work can appear under different titles. New specialisms emerge faster than formal classifications are revised. Self-employment, informal work, military service, and platform work may also be represented differently in different statistical systems.

A robust directory therefore needs:

1. A stable **standard occupation code and definition**.
2. A preferred display title plus **verified alternate titles and spelling variants**.
3. Task, skill, credential, and work-context tags.
4. Industry and specialty tags kept separate from occupation.
5. Country, language, source, version, and last-reviewed metadata.
6. An explicit “related, not necessarily equivalent” relationship where mapping is uncertain.

## 2. Global classification landscape

### Occupations

**ILO ISCO-08** is the broadest practical international reference used here. Its four-level hierarchy contains 10 major groups, 43 sub-major groups, 130 minor groups, and 436 unit groups. The ILO defines occupation by similarity of main tasks and duties and distinguishes occupation from the individual job. Its grouping uses skill level and skill specialization. This is a statistical classification, not a complete list of every employer's title.

**ESCO** provides a European multilingual occupation-and-skills classification. An occupation concept can have a preferred term and non-preferred/hidden terms in each language, a profile, and links to ISCO. This illustrates why multilingual synonyms and skills are essential for title search.

**U.S. SOC 2018** is a national statistical framework with 23 major groups and more detailed levels. The repository's local taxonomy source records 1,447 code/title records across major, minor, broad, and detailed occupations, including 867 detailed occupations. These are useful U.S. directories, not global totals.

**O\*NET** supplements U.S. occupation coding with occupation profiles and descriptors such as tasks, knowledge, skills, abilities, activities, and work context. Its database is updated regularly; it is not a global classification.

### Industries

**UN ISIC** is the international reference for classifying economic activities and is designed to support comparable economic statistics. National systems can adapt it. **NAICS 2022** is a North American system, with country-specific detail at the national level. The repository's taxonomy note records 20 NAICS sectors, 689 five-digit industry records, and 1,012 U.S. six-digit national-industry records. Those detailed levels should not be mixed or presented as a global sector list.

### Recommended relationship between the systems

| Use case | Primary reference | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-country occupation grouping | ISCO-08 | Not every local title maps one-to-one |
| European role names and skills | ESCO, with ISCO links | Regional coverage and terminology |
| U.S. occupation directory | SOC 2018 | U.S. statistical grouping, not employer leveling |
| U.S. tasks and capability descriptions | O\*NET | U.S.-oriented data and occupational scope |
| Cross-country industry comparison | UN ISIC | Local implementation may differ |
| U.S./North American industry browsing | NAICS 2022 | NAICS is not an international industry directory |

## 6. How recruiters generally treat roles and candidates

### Common selection sequence

Recruiting commonly begins with defining the vacancy and its must-haves, attracting candidates, reviewing applications, assessing, interviewing, checking references/credentials as relevant, and making an appointment. The recruiter is often coordinating a process designed with the hiring manager; the recruiter may not be the person who makes the final technical or business judgment.

In practice, a recruiter may:

1. Translate the hiring manager's request into searchable criteria: function, seniority, location, domain, skills, compensation, work authorization, and credentials.
2. Search internal applicant systems, professional networks, referrals, job boards, and direct sourcing channels. Exact query and ranking tools differ by employer and vendor.
3. Check for visible evidence against stated requirements. Title, employer, dates, keywords, and career progression are fast signals, but are imperfect proxies.
4. Conduct an initial screen for motivation, communication, availability, compensation alignment, location, authorization, and a coherent account of fit.
5. Refer candidates to the hiring manager or assessment stages, where work samples, technical interviews, case exercises, structured interviews, or practical demonstrations may be used.
6. Coordinate references, qualifications, background or eligibility checks, and offers when applicable and lawful in the jurisdiction.

Recruitment should not be described as a single universal “ATS score” or a guaranteed title match. Employer workflows vary. Screening tools and tests should relate to the job and be used consistently with applicable law; the legal details vary by jurisdiction.

### Role-family evaluation patterns

These are common tendencies, not universal rules:

| Role family | What may carry the most weight | Candidate evidence that makes fit legible |
|---|---|---|
| Executive and senior management | Scale of remit, strategy, financial accountability, change, governance, leadership, stakeholder trust | Size and type of organization/team/budget; decisions made; before/after outcomes; board/executive exposure |
| Business, finance, and operations | Accuracy, controls, commercial judgment, process, reporting, delivery | Revenue/cost/risk outcomes; systems; controls; transaction or portfolio scale; relevant standards |
| Technology and data | Technical depth, architecture, reliability, security, product impact, collaboration | Projects and systems personally owned; scale, constraints, tools, incidents, measurable performance; code or portfolio where appropriate |
| Engineering, science, and research | Method, technical rigor, safety/quality, design or research outputs, domain fit | Projects, methods, standards, publications/patents where relevant, experimental or delivery results, professional registration |
| Healthcare and care | License/registration, scope of practice, patient/service safety, specialty and setting | Current credential and jurisdiction, caseload or setting, protocols, quality and service outcomes; protect sensitive information |
| Education and social services | Subject/domain competence, safeguarding, learner/client outcomes, regulatory context | Qualifications, population served, program outcomes, teaching or service approach, required clearances |
| Legal and compliance | Jurisdictional qualification, specialty, risk judgment, confidentiality, matter or program complexity | Admission/credentials, practice area, case/matter type, transaction or program scope, defensible results without disclosing protected details |
| Sales, account, and customer roles | Customer fit, revenue or retention, pipeline quality, service and communication | Quota context, attainment period, deal size/cycle, retention, customer outcomes; explain market and territory |

| Hospitality, personal services, and retail | Service quality, operational pace, customer handling, availability, team contribution | Guest/customer outcomes, service volume, sales or quality indicators, relevant shifts and environments |

### Industry modifies the evidence

Recruiters often value a combination of **function** and **domain**:

- A finance leader in a regulated bank may need controls, regulatory, and risk experience; a finance leader in a startup may be judged more on planning, fundraising, cash management, and building systems.
- A software engineer in health technology may need privacy, safety, interoperability, or clinical workflow familiarity; a consumer product role may emphasize experimentation, engagement, or scale.
- An operations leader in manufacturing may need plant safety, yield, equipment, and quality experience; a logistics role may emphasize network performance, service levels, and fleet/warehouse execution.
- A sales role may require different proof in enterprise software, retail, professional services, or public procurement: deal cycle, buyer type, contract structure, channel, and revenue model matter.

When a candidate lacks direct sector experience, show which parts transfer and which domain knowledge can be learned. Do not imply domain expertise from a title alone.

### Recruiter type and hiring context

- **Corporate/internal recruiters** usually coordinate a particular employer's approved vacancy, process, and stakeholder expectations.
- **Agency recruiters** may work on behalf of multiple clients; specialization, client access, urgency, and fee terms influence how they search and present candidates.
- **Executive search consultants** may use targeted research and confidential outreach for senior appointments; board exposure, track record, reputation, and fit with the mandate can matter strongly.
- **Recruiting coordinators/sourcers** often focus on scheduling, candidate communication, research, and pipeline development rather than final selection.
- **Hiring managers and technical panels** typically assess functional depth and operational fit more directly, though role and organization vary.

Candidates should ask who the recruiter represents, what part they play, whether the role is approved, what information may be shared, and what the next step is. Recruiter interest is not a job offer or guarantee.

## 7. What improves cross-market role matching

### Interpret the job beyond its title

Read the job description for:

- essential outcomes and responsibilities, not just the title;
- required versus preferred capabilities;
- level indicators such as autonomy, decision rights, scope, complexity, budget, team, and accountability;
- mandatory qualifications, licensing, language, security, or work authorization;
- the employer's domain, customer, product, geography, and operating model;
- location, working pattern, travel, shift, contract type, and compensation constraints.

Use a title family to find adjacent roles, then validate each against actual tasks and requirements. Do not assume that adjacent title, same employer, or similar keywords mean the same level.

### Build a candidate evidence profile

For each target role, prepare a short evidence set:

1. **Target:** standard function plus the relevant employer title(s).
2. **Scope:** level, team/customer/product/asset/geography/budget as appropriate.
3. **Capability:** skills and tools used, with recency and depth.
4. **Outcome:** a truthful result with baseline, timeframe, and personal contribution.
5. **Context:** industry, operating constraints, scale, and stakeholder environment.
6. **Proof:** portfolio, work sample, credential, publication, reference, or case example where suitable.
7. **Transfer:** direct experience versus adjacent experience, stated plainly.

Résumé/CV and profile titles should remain truthful. A candidate can add a clarifying functional descriptor alongside an official title, but should not replace the historical title with a more senior or different role.

## 8. Implications for KOV's directory and services

1. **Retain both existing directory families, but label their geography and standard.** The role pages are SOC 2018/U.S.-oriented. The industry pages are NAICS 2022/U.S.-oriented. Put that context where a visitor can see it.
2. **Add a global entry point, not a false global exhaustive claim.** Explain ISCO and ISIC, and that local job titles and national classification variants may not map one-to-one.
3. **Keep role and industry navigation independent.** Let users explore “what work I do” separately from “what type of employer I work for,” and allow intersection filters.
4. **Treat title variants as searchable aliases.** Preserve the standardized title/code and attach verified synonyms by country and language. Do not create a separate page for every unvalidated title.
5. **Add level and evidence guidance.** Make seniority depend on scope and accountability, not words like “lead,” “principal,” “director,” or “chief” alone.
6. **Clarify the purpose of directory pages.** A taxonomy or career information page is not evidence that KOV offers placement services in that role, industry, country, or seniority.
7. **Use role-appropriate case studies and claims.** An executive's impact metrics, a nurse's license, a designer's portfolio, and a technician's certification are not interchangeable proof formats.
8. **Review data and translations routinely.** Record source edition, retrieval/review date, code, title, level, crosswalk confidence, and local legal/credential constraints.
9. **Make recruiter explanations candidate-centered and nuanced.** Explain likely evidence and process differences without promising recruiter behavior, guaranteed interviews, automatic ATS outcomes, or placement.

## 9. Limitations and responsible use

- This report is a synthesis of classification-system documentation and recruiting guidance, not a census of every title or profession.
- The role and industry examples are representative, not exhaustive.
- Recruiting practice differs by employer, country, occupation, labor market, contract type, and legal framework. U.S. EEOC selection guidance cited here applies to U.S. federal antidiscrimination law; it is not a global rule.
- Recruiter tendencies in the role-family matrix are practical generalizations. A specific vacancy's job analysis and documented criteria take precedence.
- Credentials and professional licensing are jurisdiction-specific. Candidates should verify local recognition and authorization requirements with the relevant regulator.
- Directory data should be versioned; classifications and aliases change. Verify source editions before release and avoid implying live labor-market or salary forecasts where none are supplied.
