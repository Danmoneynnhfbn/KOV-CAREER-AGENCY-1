From Banking Leadership to Fintech: Translating Your Experience

Moving from a bank into a financial-technology company is not a single, standard career move. The work depends on the company's business model, customers, regulatory relationships, and the role's actual authority. This guide helps senior banking leaders map evidence from their own experience to a specific opportunity without assuming that every fintech is a bank, that banking experience transfers automatically, or that a role is available.

Start with the employer's actual business model

"Fintech" is a broad label, not a job description or a complete description of a company's regulatory status. A company may provide payment services, lending technology, financial software, identity tools, wealth products, or infrastructure used by banks. Some companies are themselves regulated financial institutions; others provide services to institutions that retain particular responsibilities. The legal entity, product, customer, and contractual relationships matter more than the label.

Before deciding that your background fits, identify the role's customers, revenue model, product responsibilities, decision rights, and risk ownership. For U.S. banking relationships, the Federal Reserve's SR 23-4 third-party risk management guidance applies to banking organizations supervised by the Federal Reserve. It discusses relationships with some fintech companies and says it does not impose new requirements. It is not a general rulebook for every fintech, employer, or jurisdiction.

Transferability is a hypothesis to test, not a credential. For each target role, separate what you have done directly, what is adjacent and learnable, and what would require experience you do not yet have.

Check role fit before rewriting your story

Two jobs with the same executive title can have very different remits. Compare the stated mandate with the work the organization needs done. A risk leader may be expected to establish governance, advise product teams, or own a control function; an operations leader may be accountable for service delivery, implementation, or partner performance. Do not assume the title settles scope.

  • Mandate: What decisions will the role make, and what outcomes will it be accountable for?
  • Scale: What are the product, customer, transaction, team, and geographic boundaries? Use only figures the employer shares or you can lawfully verify.
  • Operating model: Which functions sit in-house, with a bank partner, or with another provider?
  • Experience gaps: Does the position require product, technology, licensing, or market experience that your record does not demonstrate?
  • Jurisdiction: Which country and legal entities are relevant? Do not apply U.S. regulatory requirements to another market.

Build an evidence-based transition narrative

A concise career narrative should connect a real business problem to your specific contribution and the outcome. It should make the banking context understandable to a reader outside a bank while preserving the limits of your role.

  1. Name the context: Identify the business unit, customer or partner need, and constraint without disclosing confidential information.
  2. State your remit: Clarify the decision, team, budget, or process for which you were accountable; distinguish your work from the wider team's work.
  3. Explain the action: Describe what you changed, how you worked across functions, and what trade-offs you considered.
  4. Show the evidence: Use an accurate outcome, timeframe, and scale where disclosure is permitted. If a number cannot be shared, give a concrete non-confidential measure or result.
  5. Connect to the target: Explain why that experience is relevant to the specific employer and role, and identify any skill or product context you still need to learn.

A strong narrative identifies the business context, your remit, relevant stakeholders, actions, and verifiable outcomes. Connect that evidence to the role's requirements and identify any experience gaps. Use only information you can substantiate and are permitted to disclose.

For a resume or profile, lead with outcomes and scope rather than a list of banking procedures. Keep regulated or confidential details appropriately generalized, and do not claim ownership of a result simply because it occurred during your tenure. Questions to test the opportunity

  • Which customer or business problem is this role expected to solve first?
  • How are responsibilities divided among the company, any regulated partners, and service providers?
  • What decisions belong to this executive, and what is escalated to a board, risk committee, or partner?
  • What would success look like in the first year, and which measures are used to assess it?
  • Which product, technology, or jurisdiction-specific experience is essential on day one?
  • What information is public, what is confidential, and what evidence can you appropriately discuss in interviews?

These are due-diligence prompts, not legal or regulatory advice. Where a role involves regulated responsibilities, get guidance from qualified professionals familiar with the relevant entity and jurisdiction.

Common questions

Do banking executives need to become software engineers to move into fintech?

That depends on the role. Some positions require deep technical expertise; others focus on operations, risk, partnerships, finance, product leadership, or governance. Read the actual role requirements and be precise about your technical experience rather than assuming either that it is unnecessary or that every executive must code.

Is fintech unregulated?

No universal statement fits every company. Applicable rules depend on activities, entities, partners, products, and jurisdictions. The cited Federal Reserve guidance concerns banking organizations supervised by the Federal Reserve and some of their third-party relationships; it should not be generalized to all fintech companies.

Does this guide establish that a banking leader is qualified for a fintech position?

No. It offers questions for assessing a particular role and presenting substantiated experience. Employers set their own requirements, and an individual transition depends on evidence, role scope, and other factors.
